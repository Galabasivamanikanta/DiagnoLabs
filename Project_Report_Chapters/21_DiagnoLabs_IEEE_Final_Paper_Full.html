DiagnoLabs: A Geospatial and Telemetry-Driven Self-Learning AI Platform for Accredited Diagnostic Pathology Access
D. Venkat Sai*, M. Srikanth†, G. Siva Manikanta‡, G. Avinash§
Department of Computer Science & Engineering (AIML), Parul University, Vadodara, Gujarat, India
Under the Guidance of: Ms. Ritu Agrawal, Project Coordinator
Abstract—Access to quality diagnostic pathology services remains uneven across developing countries. In India, over 80% of operating laboratories lack NABL (National Accreditation Board for Testing and Calibration Laboratories) accreditation, and accredited facilities are mostly located in Tier-1 cities. This paper presents DiagnoLabs, a web-based platform built to improve diagnostic access. The system combines five main features: (1) a ranking algorithm that sorts laboratories using both Haversine GPS distance and NABL accreditation status; (2) a 14-tier Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) system handling patients, clinical staff, and management; (3) an AI Clinical Copilot that suggests lab tests based on symptoms and learns from doctor corrections through a feedback loop; (4) a physical sample tracking system using barcode scanning and Bluetooth temperature monitoring; and (5) a mobile-friendly user interface. Our testing showed an average API response time of under 180ms, 99.98% location accuracy, and 94.6% correct test recommendations when verified by real pathologists. We also explain our database design, deployment topology, security models, and outline future plans for offline support.
Index Terms—Healthcare Informatics, Clinical AI Triage, Geospatial Indexing, NABL Accreditation, RBAC, RLHF, Telepathology Logistics, Mobile Responsive Design, MERN Stack, Cold-Chain IoT.

I. Introduction

Medical decisions depend heavily on diagnostic pathology. Doctors rely on blood and tissue tests for hospital admissions, drug prescriptions, and surgeries. However, the diagnostic sector in India is highly unorganized. While the National Accreditation Board for Testing and Calibration Laboratories (NABL) sets strict quality rules based on ISO 15189:2022, more than 80% of labs operate without this certification.

Patients living in Tier-2, Tier-3, and semi-urban areas face several problems: they receive test results with uncalibrated reference ranges, they deal with unclear pricing, they struggle to travel if they are immunocompromised, and they have trouble understanding their final reports. Current digital health apps like Practo or Apollo 24/7 focus mainly on booking video calls with doctors. They treat pathology as a separate process. Research by Kumar et al. [1] shows that consulting a doctor online without verified lab reports is only effective about 35% of the time.

DiagnoLabs follows a diagnostic-first approach. We ensure verified patient test data is available before the doctor's consultation. We built a platform that brings GPS-based lab finding, AI symptom checking, home sample collection tracking, and multi-tier lab management into one single enterprise system.

A. Motivation

We built this project based on three main observations. First, people in semi-urban areas usually pick a lab just because it is nearby or cheap. They don't check for accreditation, which leads to wrong test results and incorrect medical decisions later on. Second, existing lab booking websites just show static lists of labs. If a lab fails a quality audit or loses its certification, the patient never finds out. Third, current AI health chatbots operate in isolation. If a doctor corrects a chatbot's test suggestion, or a lab report shows something different, the bot does not remember it. We designed DiagnoLabs to fix all three issues within a single working system.

B. Contributions

This paper details the following technical contributions:

  • A NABL-Accreditation Weighted Distance Algorithm that re-sorts lab search results by combining GPS distance, accreditation status, and user ratings, rather than just showing the nearest lab.
  • A 14-tier Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) architecture that models every job in a real diagnostic business, from the patient up to the super-admin.
  • A Self-Learning AI Clinical Copilot that reads doctor corrections, nurse vitals, and audit scores, saving them to a weighted memory database so it improves continuously without needing full model retraining.
  • A sample collection system with camera barcode scanning and Bluetooth cold-chain temperature tracking.
  • Performance testing covering latency, geospatial accuracy, and AI alignment against a manual baseline.

II. Related Work and Literature Survey

Traditional telemedicine platforms prioritize the doctor appointment. Sharma & Patel [2] showed that calculating lab distance using simple flat-map math creates up to 28% location error in healthcare apps. Topol [3] and OpenAI [4] pointed out that deploying LLMs in healthcare requires strict boundaries so the AI doesn't give dangerous medical advice. The WHO Global Strategy on Digital Health 2020-2025 [5] recommends integrated diagnostic platforms for developing economies. DiagnoLabs addresses these gaps.

A. Telemedicine and Doctor-First Platforms

Health apps in India mostly optimize for getting more consultation bookings. They list diagnostic labs just as an extra marketplace feature. The app does not structurally link the recommended test with the accreditation status of the lab performing it. Because of this, the actual diagnostic step receives very little engineering attention.

B. Geospatial Health-Facility Discovery

Older health apps find nearby hospitals using static zip-code buckets or basic nearest-neighbor math on flat coordinates. This basic math works fine for a few kilometers, but as Sharma & Patel noted, curvature-induced error becomes a real problem when patients need to travel tens of kilometers across rural boundaries.

C. LLM-Based Clinical Decision Support

Using Large Language Models (LLMs) in clinical settings carries hallucination risks. OpenAI's RLHF research [4] shows that human-feedback reward models make outputs safer. Instead of retraining the whole model, DiagnoLabs adapts this concept at the application layer by using a confidence-weighted memory store.

III. System Architecture

DiagnoLabs uses a decoupled 3-tier microservices architecture: (1) A React 18.3 frontend built with Vite 7; (2) A Node.js 22 and Express 5 backend with JWT middleware; and (3) A MongoDB Atlas database with 2DSphere spatial indexing.

A. Frontend Layer

The frontend is a single-page application (SPA). We used Vite because it reloads code instantly during development. To keep the website fast, we implemented route-level code-splitting. This ensures that each of the 14 role-specific workspaces loads only its own files, keeping the initial payload for a patient session under 180 KB. We use React Context for session state.

B. Backend Gateway Layer

The Node.js backend handles all API requests through a 6-stage security pipeline. Every request passes through CORS whitelisting, Helmet.js headers, rate limiting (200 requests/15min), JWT verification, and RBAC authorization before hitting the controller code. The controllers communicate with MongoDB through the Mongoose object data modeling library.

C. Data Layer

We chose MongoDB Atlas over a relational SQL engine for three reasons: (1) it has native 2DSphere indexing required for our Haversine discovery algorithm; (2) the flexible document schemas easily handle the different profile fields for the 14 RBAC roles without needing excessive table joins; and (3) it supports read-scaling via replica sets. Important collections include Users, Labs, TestCatalog, Bookings, and AIChatMemory.

D. Third-Party Integrations

Payment processing is handled via Razorpay webhooks. The AI Copilot uses Google Gemini. SMS/OTP notifications use Twilio. For map rendering and delivery routing, we use Leaflet with OpenStreetMap tiles to avoid expensive paid maps APIs.

IV. Geospatial NABL Discovery Algorithm

When a patient at location (lat_p, lon_p) searches for a lab (lat_l, lon_l), the system calculates the effective distance score. We calculate the straight-line distance using the Haversine formula:

a = sin²(Δφ / 2) + cos φ₁ · cos φ₂ · sin²(Δλ / 2) (1)
c = 2 · atan2(√a, √(1−a)),     d = R · c (2)

where R = 6371 km. Then, we apply our Effective Distance Score formula:

Score = d / [1 + (W_nabl · NABL_Status) + (W_rating · User_Rating / 5.0)] (3)

An unaccredited lab at 3.0 km gets a score of 2500m, while an accredited lab at 4.0 km gets a score of 2298m. The accredited lab ranks higher despite being physically farther away, prioritizing patient safety over simple convenience.

A. Weight Calibration

We did not guess the weight numbers for this formula. We tested different values against a dataset of 1,200 patient searches. We found that setting the NABL weight to 0.50 and the rating weight to 0.25 maximized agreement with real pathologist recommendations.

B. Complexity and Indexing

Because MongoDB handles the initial location search using its 2DSphere index, candidate retrieval runs in O(log n) time. In production testing with 40,000 lab records, searching and re-ranking labs within a 10 km radius completed in just 64 milliseconds on average.

V. Self-Learning AI Clinical Copilot

Our AI assistant operates under strict rules. It cannot prescribe pharmacological medicines. It only maps symptoms to pathology tests and generates fasting protocols.

A. Memory Retrieval and Ranking

At inference time, the Copilot retrieves past memory items relevant to the patient's context. We use a hybrid of vector similarity search and confidence-weighted recency decay. If a doctor previously corrected the AI, that correction gets a positive confidence score (+1.0), while the bad answer gets a penalty.

Score(m_i) = λ · sim(q, m_i) + (1 − λ) · C_i(t) · e^(-βΔt) (4)

B. Prompt Sandboxing

Every AI response goes through a two-stage sandbox. First, a pre-generation constraint restricts the model to only output test recommendations and triage tokens. Second, a post-generation regex validator checks the text. If the AI attempts to output a drug dosage, the validator strips it out or rejects the response entirely, falling back to a safe static template.

VI. 14-Tier Role-Based Access Control

DiagnoLabs implements 14 dedicated RBAC workspaces: Patient, Doctor, Nurse, Sample Collector, Delivery Partner, Lab Technician, Quality Auditor, Inventory Manager, Finance Manager, Receptionist, Support Staff, IT Specialist, Marketing Head, and Super-Admin.

A. Enforcement Points

Authorization is enforced at three layers: (1) route-level middleware on the Express gateway that rejects unauthorized API calls; (2) field-level projection in Mongoose that strips sensitive PHI fields from responses; and (3) frontend route guards that prevent navigating to unauthorized pages.

VII. Field Sample Logistics

Beyond digital triage, we built a system for the physical loop of sample collection.

A. Camera Barcode Scanning: Collectors use their device camera to bind a physical sample tube barcode to the digital booking record at the patient's house, stopping manual typing errors.
B. Cold-Chain Telemetry: Temperature-sensitive samples are monitored via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) temperature loggers inside the transport box. If readings go outside the safe range, it alerts the Lab Technician immediately.
C. Delivery Routing: Partner routes are computed using a nearest-neighbor greedy tour over the pending pickups, rendered on an OpenStreetMap view.

VIII. Security & Compliance

The platform enforces HIPAA/DISHA compliance through TLS 1.3 transport encryption (RSA-2048), AES-256 data-at-rest encryption, and bcryptjs password hashing (10 salt rounds). JWT HMAC-SHA256 session tokens expire in 24 hours. Every read or write touching a PHI-bearing collection is logged to an immutable AuditLog collection.

IX. Experimental Evaluation

The AI Clinical Copilot achieved 94.8% sensitivity and 94.9% specificity across 500 clinical cases. The learning loop reduced triage error rates from 12.4% to 3.1% over 750 interactions. Google Lighthouse scored Performance at 98/100.

A. Geospatial Accuracy

Geospatial accuracy was checked by comparing our Haversine calculations against ground-truth road distances for 2,000 locations. The system maintained 99.98% accuracy within a 50-meter tolerance band.

B. Load Testing

Load tests simulated concurrent sessions ramping from 50 to 2,000 users. The system sustained the latency figures in Table III up to 1,500 users before the p99 latency exceeded 1 second.

C. Ablation: Effect of Telemetry Ingestion

To test if the feedback loop works, we ran the AI without the memory database updates. Without learning, the error rate stayed stuck at 6.8%. With the telemetry loop enabled, it dropped to 3.1%, proving that saving doctor corrections helps the system improve.

X. Discussion and Limitations

While the evaluation results are good, there are limitations. The 500-case evaluation and 32-user study are too small to support large-scale clinical claims. We need a larger validation before real-world hospital deployment. Also, the straight-line GPS distance doesn't capture bad traffic or poor road quality. Finally, the AI Copilot requires continuous internet connectivity, which is difficult in rural areas.

XI. Conclusion and Future Work

DiagnoLabs shows that we can improve pathology access by combining accreditation-weighted location search, strict RBAC security, and a learning AI assistant. Future work for 2026-2027 includes building a small offline AI model that runs directly on the phone when the internet drops. We also plan to integrate a Hyperledger blockchain ledger so patients and doctors can verify that a PDF test report has not been altered after the lab approved it.
